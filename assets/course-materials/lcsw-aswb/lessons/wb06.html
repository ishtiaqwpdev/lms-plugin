How to Use This Workbook

This workbook applies crisis reasoning to the ASWB Clinical Examination. The examination may place crisis content within ethics, assessment, planning, or intervention rather than in a separate domain. Read the content in order, complete the worksheets, and answer all 10 questions before reviewing the rationales. Focus on the client or client system, immediate safety, medical stability, decision-making ability, environment, resources, access, and follow-through.

National Examination NoticeThe ASWB examination is national. Unless a question supplies a jurisdiction or legal requirement, do not import California-specific reporting, duty-to-protect, or involuntary-treatment rules. Follow the law stated in the item and use ethical clinical sequencing.Clinical and Legal Scope NoticeThe material is provided for examination preparation and professional education. It is not legal advice, clinical supervision, diagnosis, treatment, or a substitute for consultation with qualified legal, clinical, regulatory, or professional experts.

Learning Objectives

  • Apply the SAFER sequence across suicide, violence, abuse, medical, substance, disaster, self-neglect, and displacement vignettes.
  • Conduct direct, specific risk assessment and formulate acuity using safety, capacity, protective factors, environment, and access to help.
  • Integrate self-determination with immediate danger, decision-making ability, vulnerability, and any jurisdiction or legal duty supplied in the question.
  • Select the least restrictive safe response and coordinate realistic medical, crisis, protective, shelter, school, language-access, and community resources.
  • Document facts, client participation, barriers, rationale, handoff, and follow-up and eliminate distractors that teach, process, refer, or coerce before acute safety is addressed.

Why This Topic Matters

Clinical social workers often encounter crises shaped by housing, poverty, health care, trauma, discrimination, caregiving, immigration, disability, transportation, and service access. The best exam answer combines direct risk assessment with respect for the client’s choices and a realistic plan. A theoretically sound plan is not safe if the client cannot understand it, reach it, afford it, or use it.

Core Exam PrincipleAssess the person and the environment together. Choose the least restrictive response that is safe, feasible, accessible, and supported.

3. Suicide and Self-Harm

  • Ask directly when the client expresses hopelessness, a wish not to wake up, burdensomeness, unbearable pain, or other warning signs.
  • Clarify passive versus active ideation, intent, plan, access to means, preparations, recent behavior, frequency, controllability, triggers, and protective factors.
  • Assess prior attempts, nonsuicidal self-injury, substance use, psychosis, agitation, sleep loss, recent loss, pain, and access to highly lethal means.
  • Do not assume that every suicidal thought requires hospitalization or that denial of current intent eliminates risk.
  • Use a collaborative safety plan and means-safety steps when a less restrictive plan is adequate and feasible.
  • Escalate when the person cannot remain safe, is medically unstable, has imminent intent or behavior, or cannot participate in a workable plan.

4. Medical, Overdose, Intoxication, and Withdrawal Emergencies

IndicatorsFirst Priority
Drowsiness, slurred speech, “handful” of medication, altered consciousnessNotify the medical team or emergency responders immediately and continue safety assessment.
Severe alcohol or sedative withdrawal with confusion, hallucinations, tremor, or autonomic signsEmergency medical evaluation.
Overheating, chest pain, extreme agitation, severe stimulant intoxicationEmergency medical and behavioral safety response.
Stable nonacute substance concernAssess pattern, consequences, readiness, supports, and appropriate treatment or referral.

5. Self-Determination, Capacity, and Least Restrictive Care

Self-determination is not the same as automatic agreement with every stated choice. The social worker should assess immediate danger and decision-making ability, provide information and options, address barriers, and avoid coercion when the client can make an informed choice and no overriding duty applies. When confusion, dehydration, severe psychosis, or inability to meet basic needs is present, capacity and medical danger may need urgent evaluation.

Exam TrapBetter Reasoning
“The client refused, so the social worker must leave.”Assess capacity, danger, and alternatives; refusal of one service is not abandonment of care.
“Any suicidal thought requires hospitalization.”Determine acuity and use the least restrictive safe level of care.
“The professional knows what is best.”Involve the client or client system and preserve choice whenever safely possible.
“A referral solves the problem.”Evaluate access, transportation, language, cost, timing, eligibility, and follow-through.

6. Threats and Violence

A statement about wanting someone to “pay” does not automatically establish a specific legal duty or emergency disposition. Clarify target, intent, plan, means, access, history, triggers, psychosis, intoxication, impulsivity, protective factors, and immediacy. When a national question does not provide a jurisdiction, assess first and avoid importing one state’s exact duty-to-protect rule.

7. Abuse, Neglect, Exploitation, and Self-Neglect

SituationBest Direction
Suspected child abuse under a supplied reporting lawAddress immediate safety and follow the jurisdiction’s mandated-reporting procedure without investigating for proof.
Older adult confusion, dehydration, no food, uncertain medicationsAssess capacity and immediate medical danger while activating urgent evaluation.
Possible financial exploitationAssess safety and basic needs, follow applicable reporting law, and connect protective and financial resources.
Client disclosure with no supplied jurisdictionProtect safety, clarify the applicable role and law, consult appropriately, and avoid assuming a specific state procedure.

8. Intimate Partner Violence, Stalking, and Sexual Assault

  • Assess immediate danger, escalation, threats, strangulation, weapons, stalking, technology monitoring, safe housing, children, pets, and safe communication.
  • Support the adult client’s choices while developing a safety response consistent with the client’s decision and applicable law.
  • Do not contact or confront the alleged perpetrator, require a police report, or force separation without a legal basis.
  • For recent sexual assault, assess immediate safety, medical needs, safe housing, supports, and preferences for advocacy and reporting resources.
  • Avoid detailed trauma narration before stabilization and consent.

9. Homelessness, Psychosis, and Outreach Triage

When a client is responding to voices, has not eaten, and is sleeping in a dangerous location, assess current danger, medical needs, ability to care for basic needs, substance use, and willingness to accept evaluation. Offer the least restrictive safe options. Refusal of one shelter does not end the social worker’s responsibility to continue engagement, clarify barriers, and offer alternatives.

10. Disaster, Displacement, Refugee, and Discrimination Crises

12. Crisis Documentation and Collaboration

  • Record direct statements, objective observations, questions asked, responses, collateral information, and changes from baseline.
  • Document historical, dynamic, protective, and acute factors and how they support the formulation.
  • Record consultation, reports, emergency contacts, medical coordination, crisis-team involvement, and releases or legal bases for disclosure.
  • Explain client participation, the rationale for the level of care, barriers addressed, handoff status, and follow-up responsibilities.
  • Do not document conclusory labels without the facts that support them.

Worked Example 1: Housing Loss and Passive Death Wish

A client who recently lost housing says, “I cannot do this anymore. I wish I would not wake up.” Housing assistance is important, but the statement also requires direct suicide assessment before the social worker can determine the safest disposition.

Best First StepAsk directly about suicidal thoughts, intent, plan, access to means, recent behavior, and protective factors. Then integrate housing and other environmental needs into the safety and service plan.

Worked Example 2: Older Adult Refusing Help

An older adult is confused, dehydrated, without food, and unable to describe medication use, yet refuses to leave the apartment. The refusal does not end the assessment because decision-making ability and immediate medical danger are uncertain.

Best First StepAssess capacity and immediate medical risk while activating urgent medical or emergency evaluation. Long-term placement decisions follow stabilization.

Chapter Summary

  • Use SAFER to stabilize, assess directly, formulate risk in context, execute the least restrictive safe response, and reconnect and reassess.
  • Possible overdose, severe withdrawal, altered consciousness, dehydration, or other medical instability takes priority over routine psychosocial intervention.
  • Self-determination requires meaningful choice, but refusal does not end assessment when capacity, immediate danger, or basic-needs functioning is uncertain.
  • A safety plan must be usable: language, disability access, transportation, housing, cost, caregiving, technology, and service availability affect whether the plan is safe.
  • On a national ASWB item, use the jurisdiction or legal rule supplied in the stem and avoid importing one state’s exact reporting, duty-to-protect, or involuntary-treatment procedure.

My Crisis Review Checklist

  • Did I identify immediate danger, medical instability, or a required protective action before routine treatment?
  • Did I ask directly about ideation, intent, plan, means, preparations, timing, target, behavior, and protective factors?
  • Did I distinguish facts that require more assessment from facts that already require action?
  • Did I select the least restrictive option that is actually safe, feasible, accessible, and supported?
  • Did I separate legal duties, emergency coordination, clinical support, documentation, and follow-up?
