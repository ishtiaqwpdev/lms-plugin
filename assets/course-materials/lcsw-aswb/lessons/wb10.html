How to Use This Workbook

This workbook develops the applied systems reasoning used when clients need coordinated clinical, medical, educational, legal, housing, benefit, disability, family, or community responses. Read each section, practice mapping responsibilities and barriers, complete the worksheet, and answer the knowledge check before reviewing the rationales.

Core Exam QuestionWhat should the social worker coordinate or advocate for next, with whom, under what authority, and how will access and follow-through be confirmed?
Blueprint NoteFor Clinical exams administered on or after August 3, 2026, the ASWB outline assigns 36% to Values and Ethics, 32% to Assessment and Planning, and 32% to Intervention and Practice. Intervention questions emphasize applied reasoning, client or client-system fit, ethical sequencing, and the use of both three- and four-option formats.

Learning Objectives

  • Use person-in-environment assessment to prioritize safety, health, housing, benefits, access, functioning, and client-defined goals and translate them into an accountable case-management plan.
  • Map clients, families, providers, agencies, benefits, services, eligibility rules, environmental barriers, and community systems and clarify who has authority and responsibility for each next step.
  • Select the least intrusive effective level of individual, organizational, systems, or community advocacy while preserving self-determination, informed participation, privacy, and attention to power and risk.
  • Match resources and referrals to need, eligibility, availability, cost, transportation, language, disability, technology, culture, and client preference and use warm handoffs and follow-up to protect continuity.
  • Document consent, role, responsibility, barriers, actions, deadlines, and outcomes and revise coordination when access, follow-through, or service effectiveness is inadequate.

Why This Topic Matters

ASWB-style questions often present several helpful services or professionals. The best answer is usually the next coordinated step that matches acuity, client choice, access, authority, and role. A referral is not complete when the name of an agency is provided. The social worker must think through eligibility, accessibility, consent, responsibility, and follow-through.

StepApplied Question

Memory AidBRIDGE turns a referral into an accountable, client-centered coordination process.

2. Case Management as Integrated Clinical Practice

Case management links assessment, planning, resource mobilization, counseling, advocacy, monitoring, and continuity. It is not separate from clinical work when environmental barriers, health conditions, caregiving, poverty, discrimination, or organizational systems maintain distress. The social worker continues to define the purpose of each action and how it supports the client’s goals and functioning.

FunctionExamples
AssessmentNeeds, strengths, risk, functioning, supports, eligibility, access, and client priorities.
PlanningGoals, sequencing, service intensity, responsible parties, consent, and review dates.
Linkage and coordinationWarm handoffs, records with authorization, meetings, navigation, and role clarification.
Monitoring and evaluationAccess, attendance, outcomes, barriers, satisfaction, continuity, and plan revision.

3. Prioritization and Triage Across Systems

The social worker prioritizes immediate safety and medical needs, basic survival, time-sensitive rights or benefits, and conditions that block all other care. Prioritization does not mean that only one problem matters. It means sequencing the plan so urgent needs are stabilized while longer-term clinical and social goals remain visible.

Priority QuestionExamples
Immediate safety or medical urgency?Suicide risk, abuse, overdose, dangerous withdrawal, acute illness, unsafe shelter, or imminent violence.
Basic needs blocking care?Housing, food, transportation, medication, identification, childcare, communication, or disability access.
Time-sensitive system requirement?Benefit deadline, discharge, school meeting, court date, placement decision, or expiring authorization.
Longer-term recovery and functioning?Therapy, employment, education, family support, community integration, and prevention.

4. Client-System and Service Mapping

A service map identifies the client’s natural supports, family or caregivers, health and behavioral-health providers, schools, employers, benefit systems, housing, legal resources, community organizations, and informal supports. The social worker looks for gaps, duplication, conflicting plans, inaccessible requirements, and unclear responsibility. The map should remain understandable to the client.

Mapping CheckWho is involved, what is each person or agency responsible for, what barrier exists, and who owns the next step?

5. Resource Matching, Eligibility, and Accessibility

A resource is useful only when it matches the need and the client can realistically access it. The social worker checks eligibility, documentation, language, disability access, cultural responsiveness, cost, transportation, technology, hours, waitlists, childcare, location, safety, and client preference. Missed appointments or incomplete applications are assessment data, not automatic evidence of poor motivation.

Resource QuestionWhat to Verify
Does it match the need?Function, specialty, intensity, population, urgency, and service quality.
Can the client qualify?Eligibility, documentation, referral, insurance, location, age, income, and status requirements.
Can the client use it?Language, literacy, disability access, transportation, technology, childcare, cost, hours, privacy, and safety.
Does the client choose it?Understanding, preferences, risks, cultural fit, and alternatives.

6. Warm Handoffs and Navigation

A warm handoff may include obtaining authorization, contacting the receiving provider with the client, sending limited relevant information, scheduling or confirming the first step, preparing the client for what to expect, and checking whether entry occurred. The intensity of the handoff should match the client’s need, urgency, capacity, and preference.

  1. Clarify the need and why the referral is recommended.
  2. Discuss options and obtain informed participation and necessary authorization.
  3. Verify fit, eligibility, access, and expected wait time.
  4. Connect the client with the receiving service and clarify responsibility.
  5. Confirm whether contact and entry occurred.
  6. Revise the plan if the service is unavailable, inaccessible, or ineffective.

7. Advocacy at the Correct Level

Advocacy may occur with a provider, agency, school, employer, insurer, benefit system, court-related service, community organization, or policy process. The social worker begins with the client’s goal and the barrier, assesses risk and power, and chooses the least intrusive effective strategy. Advocacy should expand client voice and access rather than substitute professional control for client participation.

LevelPossible Actions
Case advocacyClarify a decision, request accommodation, correct an error, support an appeal, prepare the client, or negotiate access.
Organizational advocacyAddress repeated barriers, inaccessible procedures, discriminatory practices, or coordination failures within an agency.
Community or policy advocacySupport affected groups in identifying priorities, building leadership, gathering evidence, engaging decision makers, and evaluating change.

8. Interdisciplinary Collaboration and Role Clarity

Interdisciplinary work requires clarity about professional roles, authority, scope, decision-making responsibility, and client participation. The social worker translates systems into understandable language, identifies disagreement, prevents tasks from being assumed or duplicated, and documents the plan. Collaboration does not authorize unlimited information sharing.

BeforeDuringAfter
Clarify the client’s goals, consent, concerns, preferred participants, and the social worker’s role.Use understandable language, name role or plan confusion, invite client participation, and assign responsibility.Document decisions, disclosures, tasks, deadlines, barriers, and the next review point.

9. Consent, Confidentiality, and Minimum Necessary Information

The social worker clarifies the purpose of coordination, the client’s authorization, the recipient, the information needed, and the time period. When legal, safety, organizational, or capacity rules affect disclosure, the social worker applies the governing requirement and shares only what is necessary for the defined purpose. Clients should understand who is involved and what information will be exchanged whenever possible.

Disclosure RuleCollaboration is a purpose, not blanket permission. Define authority, recipient, relevance, and limits.

10. Documentation, Accountability, and Follow-Through

Strong coordination documentation records the client’s goals and participation, assessed needs and barriers, consent and information-sharing limits, referrals and contacts, assigned responsibilities, deadlines, outcomes, and plan revisions. Phrases such as “resources provided” or “team consulted” are weak when the record does not show what occurred or who owns the next step.

Weak EntryStronger Entry
“Housing resources provided.”Document the housing need, options discussed, eligibility and access barriers, client choice, contact made, and follow-up date.
“Team notified.”Document who was contacted, the purpose and authority, relevant information shared, response, assigned task, and deadline.
“Client noncompliant.”Describe the observable event, client explanation, barriers assessed, options offered, and revised plan.
“Referral complete.”Confirm whether the receiving service accepted the referral and whether the client accessed the service.

12. Immigration, Refugee, Language, and Legal-Service Coordination

Clients may face language barriers, fear of systems, legal uncertainty, documentation problems, family separation, exploitation, or trauma. The social worker provides accurate information within competence, arranges qualified language access, identifies reputable legal or benefits resources, assesses safety and practical barriers, and supports the client’s informed decisions. The social worker does not give legal advice outside training or make absolute promises about consequences.

13. Practice Across Child Welfare, Schools, Health, Corrections, and Community Settings

The same coordination principles apply across settings, but authority, rights, roles, and timelines differ. The social worker must clarify whether participation is voluntary or mandated, what decisions the social worker can influence, how the client or family can participate, and which jurisdiction-specific rules govern. The examination generally rewards role clarity, client understanding, and the least restrictive effective response.

14. Community Participation and Systems Change

Community practice begins with listening to affected people, identifying power and decision makers, assessing access and unintended risks, and supporting meaningful leadership. Social workers may facilitate meetings, gather data, build coalitions, support communication, or advocate for policy and environmental change. The process should not use community members only to endorse a plan professionals have already designed.

15. When the Service Plan Is Not Working

1. Reassess immediate safety, medical urgency, basic needs, and level of care.

2. Ask the client what happened and whether the plan felt understandable, respectful, feasible, and useful.

3. Verify eligibility, waitlists, language, literacy, disability, transportation, cost, technology, childcare, documentation, and scheduling barriers.

4. Clarify roles, consent, information sharing, and responsibility across providers.

5. Modify the resource, advocacy strategy, service intensity, or handoff.

6. Set a new review point and document outcomes and client participation.

Common Exam Traps

TrapCorrection

Worked Example 1: Medical and Housing Coordination

A client with diabetes and depression is discharged to temporary housing without transportation or medication access. The strongest response is not simply a therapy appointment or a housing list. The social worker prioritizes medical and housing safety, clarifies the client’s goals and consent, maps responsible providers, addresses transportation and pharmacy barriers, completes accessible handoffs, and schedules follow-up.

Best ReasoningCoordinate the barriers that prevent the clinical plan from working.

Worked Example 2: School Team and Parent Participation

A caregiver is confused by an upcoming school meeting and believes decisions have already been made. The social worker helps clarify the meeting’s purpose, the caregiver’s goals and questions, the social worker’s role, available supports, and how the caregiver can participate. After the meeting, the social worker confirms responsibilities, timelines, and access to the agreed services.

Best ReasoningRole clarity and understandable participation are intervention tasks, not administrative extras.

Chapter Summary

  • Case management links assessment, planning, resources, coordination, monitoring, and continuity across time and systems; it is more than giving a referral list.
  • Use BRIDGE: identify the barrier, resource fit, informed participation, defined responsibility, guided handoff, and evaluation of access and outcomes.
  • Advocacy begins by clarifying the client’s goal, the barrier, power and risk, desired participation, and the least intrusive effective level of action.
  • Collaboration requires explicit roles, authority, consent, relevant information limits, deadlines, and ownership of the next step so responsibility does not disappear between systems.
  • When a service is not used, assess accessibility, eligibility, literacy, language, transportation, childcare, technology, discrimination, and other barriers before labeling the client unmotivated.
